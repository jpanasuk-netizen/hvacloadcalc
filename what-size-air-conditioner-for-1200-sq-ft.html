What Size Air Conditioner Do I Need for 1,200 Sq Ft?

Last updated: October 7, 2026

1,200 sq ft sits on the line between 21,000 and 23,000 BTU/hr, and it is still a room-AC question.

Quick answer: 1,200 sq ft is the edge between 21,000 BTU/hr (1,000 up to 1,200 sq ft) and 23,000 BTU/hr (1,200 up to 1,400 sq ft). Choose 21,000 for heavy shade. Choose 23,000 for a very sunny space or for more than two people regularly in the area. The chart is a room air conditioner chart either way.

How to size it

The rows differ by 2,000 BTU/hr. Shade on the lower row moves the target by 2,100 BTU/hr, which is slightly more than the gap, so a shaded space that correctly starts at 21,000 finishes at 18,900 BTU/hr. Do not average the two rows. The chart does not publish a capacity between them.

10% of 21,000 BTU/hr is 2,100 BTU/hr (21,000 × 10%).

10% of 23,000 BTU/hr is 2,300 BTU/hr (23,000 × 10%).

Sunny upper row: 23,000 + 10% = 25,300 BTU/hr. One person beyond two on that upper row: 23,000 + 600 = 23,600 BTU/hr. Kitchen on the lower row: 21,000 + 4,000 = 25,000 BTU/hr. Kitchen on the upper row: 23,000 + 4,000 = 27,000 BTU/hr. The kitchen figure is for a unit used in a kitchen, which at 1,200 sq ft usually means the area includes the kitchen, not that the entire floor is a kitchen. Apply it only when that use is real.

Ceiling basis remains 8-foot. ENERGY STAR's higher-ceiling sentence still says you may want a room air conditioner with a higher CADR. It does not add a sq-ft exception at 1,200.

Worked example: 1,200 sq ft through this site's calculator

The chart above is ENERGY STAR's room air conditioner table. The room BTU calculator on this site is a separate planning method with its own factors, listed on the methodology page: square feet × 25 BTU/hr, times ceiling height ÷ 8, times a sun factor and an insulation factor, plus 600 BTU/hr per window, plus 10%, rounded up to the next 500. Those factors belong to this calculator. They are not ENERGY STAR or Manual J figures.

Example inputs: a 1,200 sq ft single-story space with seven windows, an 8 ft ceiling, average sun, and average insulation.

  1. Floor area: 1,200 × 25 = 30,000 BTU/hr.
  2. Windows: 7 × 600 = 4,200 BTU/hr. Running total: 34,200.
  3. Add 10%: 34,200 × 1.10 = 37,620 BTU/hr.
  4. Round up to the next 500: 38,000 BTU/hr, or 3.2 tons.

That is above the largest single head in the calculator's mini-split tab (36k), so it points to zones: about 19,000 BTU/hr each with two zones, or 13,000 with three. Open this example in the calculator and change any input.

That is 31.7 BTU/hr per sq ft, against the ENERGY STAR rows of 21,000 and 23,000 BTU/hr for 1,200 sq ft. The two methods answer different questions. The calculator adds window load and a 10% margin to a whole-space estimate. The ENERGY STAR chart sizes a room air conditioner for the area it cools. Ceiling height and insulation move the result more than one window does, because they multiply the whole floor-area load. ENERGY STAR handles ceilings differently: its capacities assume an 8-foot ceiling, and for higher ceilings it says you may want a unit with a higher CADR. It does not publish a per-foot adder.

Occupants, kitchen, and climate at 1,200 sq ft

The calculator has no occupant input, and cooking heat is not an input either. ENERGY STAR's adjustments cover both as fixed amounts: 600 BTU/hr for each person beyond two who regularly uses the space, and 4,000 BTU/hr for a unit used in a kitchen. Because they are fixed, their weight depends on size. At 1,200 sq ft the kitchen adder is 19% of 21,000 and 17% of 23,000. Two extra regular occupants add 1,200 BTU/hr, which is 6% of 21,000 and 5% of 23,000.

Neither the chart nor the calculator has a climate input. DOE describes one ton (12,000 Btu) per 400 to 500 sq ft as an old estimate that "does not take into account local climate and how well a house is sealed and insulated," and says that in a very efficient house one ton can condition 800 to 1,000 sq ft. For 1,200 sq ft, those two statements work out to 2.4 to 3 tons (28,800 to 36,000 BTU/hr) and 1.2 to 1.5 tons (14,400 to 18,000 BTU/hr). Manual J brings climate in through outdoor design conditions, taken from Manual J Tables 1A/1B or ASHRAE weather data (ACCA). DOE adds that correct sizing is especially important in humid climates, where short-cycling leads to poor humidity control (DOE fact sheet).

Common mistakes at 1,200 sq ft

  • Treating the room-AC row as a central-system size. DOE says to insist that your contractor use ACCA Manual J to size heating and cooling equipment. The 21,000 and 23,000 rows are room air conditioner capacities.
  • Not asking for the load calculation in writing. DOE's right-sizing fact sheet says oversizing is very common: in one large survey, nearly 40 percent of contractors said they oversized equipment on purpose. Its advice is to insist on documented Manual J calculations and to use Manual S to select the equipment.
  • Putting one large head on a multi-room plan. The worked example above comes out above this site's largest single head (36,000 BTU/hr), so the calculator's mini-split tab points to zones. Size each zone from its own floor area.
  • Averaging the two rows. ENERGY STAR publishes 21,000 and 23,000 and nothing between them. Pick the row from the measured area, then adjust it.

Frequently asked questions

What size air conditioner for 1,200 sq ft?

1,000 up to 1,200 sq ft is 21,000 BTU/hr, and 1,200 up to 1,400 sq ft is 23,000 BTU/hr. Heavy shade points at 21,000. A very sunny space or more than two people regularly points at 23,000.

How big is the step between those two rows?

23,000 − 21,000 = 2,000 BTU/hr. Ten percent of 21,000 is 2,100, so a shaded lower-row target is 18,900 BTU/hr. Ten percent of 23,000 is 2,300, so a sunny upper-row target is 25,300 BTU/hr.

Should a 1,200 sq ft home buy a window unit from this chart?
