Best mini split for cold climates

Published November 10, 2026

A cold-climate mini split is the one whose spec sheet clears ENERGY STAR’s Cold Climate lines. This page does not rank brands.

Quick answer: To earn ENERGY STAR's Cold Climate label, a heat pump, including a ductless mini split, needs a COP of at least 1.75 at 5°F and must keep at least 70% of its 47°F heating capacity at 5°F. So a 12,000 Btu/h unit has to deliver at least 8,400 Btu/h at 5°F to qualify.

8,400 is 70% of 12,000 (derived). The 1.75 COP and the 70% capacity line are from ENERGY STAR Heat Pump Key Product Criteria, read November 10, 2026. The same 1.75 COP at 5°F and 70% heating capacity at 5°F compared with 47°F are restated for cold-climate heat pumps in the ENERGY STAR Most Efficient 2025 cover letter (December 4, 2024), also read November 10, 2026.

Check a spec sheet

Type the three heating figures from the model’s extended performance data. The checker compares them with the two low-ambient lines above. It does not look up a brand, and it does not size the room.

The example sets 12,000 Btu/h at 47°F, 8,400 Btu/h at 5°F, and a COP of 1.75. 8,400 is 70% of 12,000. It is not a model.

What the Cold Climate label actually requires

ENERGY STAR’s key product criteria split the Cold Climate designation into efficiency floors and a low-ambient test. A ductless mini split is the non-ducted split row.

Cold Climate efficiency floors, ENERGY STAR Heat Pump Key Product Criteria, read November 10, 2026.
EquipmentHSPF2SEER2
Split systems, non-ductedat least 8.5at least 15.2

The same page’s ordinary heat-pump floor for split systems is at least 7.8 HSPF2, at least 15.2 SEER2, and at least 11.0 EER2. Cold Climate raises the HSPF2 floor. It does not lower the 15.2 SEER2 floor.

Low-ambient performance, same page, for the Cold Climate designation:

  • COP at 5°F of at least 1.75.
  • Heating capacity at 5°F at least 70% of the nominal heating capacity at 47°F.
  • A controls verification procedure, so those 5°F results come from the controls the unit would use in a home. ENERGY STAR accepts its own procedure or a procedure DOE has adopted into the Code of Federal Regulations.

Passing the two numbers in the checker is the capacity and COP part only. HSPF2, SEER2, and the controls procedure are still required, and they are not inputs above. Certified cooling capacity, SEER2, EER2, and HSPF2 for single-zone models already copied onto this site are on the ENERGY STAR mini-split chart. That chart does not list 5°F heating capacity.

The 12,000 Btu/h example

Take a heating capacity of 12,000 Btu/h at 47°F. Seventy percent of 12,000 is 8,400 (derived: 0.70 × 12,000). A unit at that nameplate has to deliver at least 8,400 Btu/h at 5°F, and its COP at 5°F has to be at least 1.75. 12,000 is an example so the percent has a number. It is also one of the head sizes on the mini-split room-size page. It is not a recommendation of a brand.

Most Efficient 2025 is a higher bar

The December 4, 2024 Most Efficient 2025 cover letter keeps 1.75 COP at 5°F and 70% heating capacity at 5°F versus 47°F for cold-climate heat pumps, and it adds recognition criteria on top. Those criteria are not the Cold Climate label floors in the table above.

ENERGY STAR Most Efficient 2025 air-source heat pump recognition criteria, from the December 4, 2024 cover letter.

The same letter sets a different capacity backstop for heat pumps that are not cold-climate: COP at least 1.75 at 5°F, and heating capacity at 5°F at least 45% of the 47°F capacity. 45% is that non-cold-climate Most Efficient backstop. The cold-climate line stays 70%.

Then size the room

The 5°F capacity says how the machine behaves in the cold. It does not say how much cooling the room needs. The calculator below is this site’s room-load function, the same one on the homepage calculator and described on BTU per square foot: 25 BTU/hr per square foot, times ceiling height ÷ 8, times a sun factor (shaded 0.90, average 1.00, very sunny 1.15) and an insulation factor (good 0.85, average 1.00, poor 1.25), plus 600 BTU/hr per window, then a 10% margin, rounded up to the next 500. Heads are 9,000, 12,000, 15,000, 18,000, 24,000, 30,000, and 36,000 BTU/hr. Those factors are this calculator’s, not ENERGY STAR’s room chart. The room chart is on ENERGY STAR sizing data.

Heating load versus cooling load, including DOE’s 15% and 25% limits against a Manual J load, is heat pump sizing. Do not add those percents to the number above. This result is a planning estimate, not a Manual J heating load, and it is not the 47°F or 5°F capacity on the spec sheet.

What this page will not pick

No brand, no model, and no “best buy.” Two units can both clear 1.75 and 70% and still differ in noise, line-set length, and the HSPF2 on the nameplate. Read those on the spec sheet and on the SEER2 chart. If the room load is the only number you have, start with mini-split size by room, then come back and test the heating rows.

Honest label: rule-of-thumb sizing is a planning estimate, not a substitute for a full ACCA Manual J. Extreme climates, unusual envelopes, and multi-zone diversity deserve a real load calculation before you spend thousands.
