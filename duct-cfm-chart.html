Duct CFM: the BTU ÷ (ΔT × 1.08) guide

One formula turns equipment size into the airflow your ducts must carry — with the worked numbers for every common system size.

Quick answer: Size ducts at about 400 CFM per ton of cooling — 2 tons ≈ 800 CFM, 3 tons ≈ 1,200 CFM — or compute exactly with CFM = BTU/hr ÷ (ΔT × 1.08). Use the chart below for your system size.

The formula

CFM = BTU/hr ÷ (ΔT × 1.08). The 1.08 constant is air's heat-carrying capacity at sea level (0.075 lb/cu ft density × 0.24 BTU/lb-°F × 60 min/hr). ΔT is the temperature split between supply and return air — the amount the equipment heats or cools the air in one pass.

Worked example: a 2-ton (24,000 BTU/hr) AC at a typical cooling ΔT of 27–28°F: CFM = 24,000 ÷ (28 × 1.08) ≈ 794 CFM — which is exactly where the industry shorthand of ~400 CFM per ton comes from. Setups with a smaller split (20°F) run more CFM per ton; drier-coil designs with a bigger split run less. Your equipment's spec sheet and measured split settle it.

Heating with a gas furnace? ΔT is much larger — 40–60°F is normal, so a 60,000 BTU/hr furnace at ΔT 50 needs only 1,111 CFM. Heat pumps sit near 20–25°F in heating.

Per-run airflow

Divide total CFM by the number of supply runs to sanity-check the branch layout. At 1,200 CFM across 8 runs, each branch carries 150 CFM — typical residential branches at 6–7 inch round carry 100–150 CFM. If your per-run number forces 8-inch branches everywhere, the trunk is starved or the run count is too low.

Diagnosing an existing system

Measure supply and return temps a few feet from the equipment with a probe thermometer. Cooling split much under 16°F usually means low airflow (dirty coil/filter, undersized returns); much over 22°F on AC suggests low charge or, in humid climates, deliberately low airflow for dehumidification. The formula runs both directions — measured CFM × ΔT × 1.08 gives you delivered BTU/hr.

Worked example on this site’s duct tab

The duct tab calls ductCFM: BTU/hr ÷ (ΔT × 1.08). One ton is 12,000 BTU/hr, so a 3-ton cooling rate is 36,000 BTU/hr and a 2-ton rate is 24,000 BTU/hr. At a 20°F supply-to-return split, ΔT × 1.08 is 21.6. ductCFM(36000, 20) is 36,000 ÷ 21.6 = 1,666.666… CFM, and the ΔT 20 column above rounds that to 1,667. ductCFM(24000, 20) is 24,000 ÷ 21.6 = 1,111.111… CFM, which that column rounds to 1,111. Split the 2-ton result across 6 supply runs and each run is 1,111.111 ÷ 6 = 185.185 CFM.

Open the calculator, choose Duct CFM, and enter 36000 with a ΔT of 20, or 24000 with a ΔT of 20. The result card shows the unrounded quotient. Square footage does not belong in that tab. The room-BTU tab is where square footage, ceiling height, windows, sun, and insulation are entered. CFM starts from a BTU/hr figure you already have.

The ~400 CFM per ton shorthand on this page is the 28°F example already shown above, not the 20°F column. 24,000 ÷ (28 × 1.08) = 24,000 ÷ 30.24 ≈ 794 CFM, and 794 ÷ 2 ≈ 397 CFM per ton. At ΔT 20 the same 24,000 BTU/hr is 1,111 CFM, about 556 CFM per ton. Using 400 CFM per ton when the split is 20°F understates the airflow the formula returns.

Common mistake

Reading a duct diameter off the CFM. The formula returns an airflow. Diameter, friction rate, and available static pressure are ACCA Manual D. The approximate branch table on this page is a sanity check, not a design, and it is not a Manual D friction-rate table. The ASHRAE handbook is the engineering reference for the air-side quantities. It does not replace a measurement of the equipment’s external static pressure, and the blower still has to be able to move the CFM against that pressure.

The other mistake is typing the room’s square footage into the duct tab and treating the result as a system size. Size the equipment on the room-BTU tab, or from a Manual J, then bring that BTU/hr here.

Frequently asked questions

How many CFM is a 3-ton system at a 20°F split?

ductCFM(36000, 20) is 36,000 ÷ (20 × 1.08) = 1,666.666… CFM. This page’s chart rounds that to 1,667. One ton is 12,000 BTU/hr, so 3 tons is 36,000 BTU/hr.

Does that CFM pick the duct diameter?

No. ACCA Manual D sizes the duct from friction rate, material, and available static pressure. The CFM is the airflow target. A diameter guessed from CFM alone is not a Manual D.

Why is 400 CFM per ton not the same as the 20°F column?

400 CFM per ton on this page is the 28°F worked example: 24,000 ÷ (28 × 1.08) ≈ 794 CFM, or about 397 CFM per ton. At a 20°F split the same 24,000 BTU/hr is 1,111 CFM. The chart’s three columns are three different splits, not three ways of writing 400.
