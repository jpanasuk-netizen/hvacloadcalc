What Does BTU Stand For?

The letters name a unit of heat. The number on an air conditioner is a rate: British thermal units per hour.

Built by Jeremy Panasuk. Published October 3, 2026.

Quick answer: BTU stands for British thermal unit. ENERGY STAR lists room-air-conditioner capacity in British thermal units (BTUs) per hour. The U.S. Department of Energy's Guide to Home Heating and Cooling equates one ton of cooling with 12,000 Btu. A nameplate that says "5,000 BTU" is using the short form of 5,000 BTU per hour.

A rate, not a tank of cold

ENERGY STAR's room air conditioner chart is explicit about the unit: cooling capacity is shown in British thermal units per hour, and the chart itself is calculated for an 8-foot ceiling. The "per hour" is the part quotes drop. An air conditioner does not hold 5,000 BTU the way a bottle holds water. Each hour it is running, it is rated to move that much heat out of the room. Stop the compressor and the hourly rate stops with it.

That is also why a ton is not the shipping weight of the condenser. DOE's heating and cooling guide uses "one ton (12,000 Btu)" as the cooling quantity in its old square-foot estimate. Divide any BTU/hr rating by 12,000 to state it in tons. A 12,000 BTU/hr unit is 1 ton. A 5,000 BTU/hr unit is 5,000 ÷ 12,000 = 0.42 ton. This site's calculator uses that same 12,000 BTU/hr per ton when it prints a tonnage next to a room result.

DOE also warns that the old shortcut — one ton for each 400 to 500 square feet — ignores climate and how tight the house is. In a very efficient house, the same guide says one ton can condition 800 to 1,000 square feet. The abbreviation is not the sizing method. The load calculator is a planning pass with its own factors. It is not a Manual J.

Worked example: 140 sq ft, one window, 8 ft ceiling

These inputs go through this site's room-load function, the same one on the calculator's Room BTU tab. They are not an ENERGY STAR formula. The chart is shown beside them so the two outputs can be compared.

  • Floor area: 140 sq ft. ENERGY STAR's row for 100 up to 150 sq ft is 5,000 BTU/hr.
  • Ceiling: 8 ft, so the calculator's height factor is 8 ÷ 8 = 1.
  • Sun: average, factor 1.00. Insulation: average, factor 1.00.
  • Windows: 1. This calculator adds 600 BTU/hr per window before the safety margin. That 600 is this calculator's window term. ENERGY STAR's separate 600 BTU figure is an extra-person adder, not a window adder. Do not mix them.

Shell = 140 × 25 × 1 × 1.00 × 1.00 = 3,500 BTU/hr. Glass = 1 × 600 = 600. Subtotal = 4,100. The 10% safety margin makes the raw load 4,100 × 1.10 = 4,510 BTU/hr. The calculator then rounds up to the next 500, which is 5,000 BTU/hr.

So this particular room prints 5,000 BTU/hr, the same integer as the ENERGY STAR row for 100 up to 150 sq ft. The paths are not the same. The chart did not add a window or a 10% margin; the calculator did, and rounding landed on the chart's number. Change the window count or the ceiling and the calculator moves while the chart row, which only asks for floor area, does not. At 5,000 BTU/hr the tonnage is 5,000 ÷ 12,000 = 0.42 ton.

Common mistake

The usual mix-up is shopping the biggest BTU number in the budget. ENERGY STAR says a unit that is too big cools the room before it has removed the humidity, so the air can feel damp and clammy, and that the most efficient room air conditioner is the one sized to the floor area. Reading "BTU" as "more is safer" treats a rate as a score. The other mix-up is comparing a tonnage quote to a BTU/hr nameplate without dividing by 12,000. Two tons is 24,000 Btu/hr on DOE's definition, not 2,000.

Limits, and when to call a pro

Nothing on this page is an ACCA Manual J. DOE's guide says to insist that the contractor use Manual J for the proper size of heating and cooling equipment, because the old ton-per-area estimate skips climate and the envelope. Use the room calculator to see how ceiling, sun, insulation, and windows move a planning number. For a central system, or any house that is very tight or very leaky, have a contractor run Manual J before you buy. Room-air-conditioner shopping still starts from the ENERGY STAR chart, then the adjustments that chart actually publishes. The BTU to kW converter is the heating-side unit change, and it is not this cooling rate.

Frequently asked questions

What does BTU stand for on an air conditioner?

BTU stands for British thermal unit. ENERGY STAR sizes room air conditioners in British thermal units per hour, which is a rate of heat removal, not a lump of energy stored in the box.

How many BTU are in a ton of cooling?

DOE's Guide to Home Heating and Cooling states that one ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu. A 5,000 BTU/hr room unit is 5,000 ÷ 12,000 = 0.42 ton.

Is a higher BTU number always better?

No. ENERGY STAR says a room air conditioner that is too large cools the room before it removes the humidity, so the room can feel damp and clammy. Match the BTU/hr rate to the room.
