How many BTU per square foot do you need?

20–30 BTU/hr per square foot, 25 as the honest middle — and five adjustments that decide which end of the range you're actually on.

Quick answer: Start at 25 BTU/hr per square foot (range 20–30) for a typical 8-foot-ceiling room — a 12×12 room lands at 4,500–5,000 BTU after adjustments. Run the calculator below for your exact room.

The rule, and why it's a range

Base load = square footage × 25 BTU/hr. The published range is 20–30: the low end assumes a tight, shaded, well-insulated envelope; the high end assumes a sunny, leaky, under-insulated one in a hot climate. Most real rooms deserve 25, then get adjusted — not defaulted to 30 "to be safe."

Per-square-foot rules are a planning tool, not a Manual J replacement. They work because the error bars are known and adjustable — which is exactly what the next section does.

Heating uses different math

Cooling rules-of-thumb don't transfer to heat. Electric baseboard heating sizes by watts (10 W/sq ft as the cold-climate baseline) — that's a separate calculation in the baseboard watts guide. Heat pumps get sized to the cooling load first and checked against your region's design temperature second, because a unit that nails July can still fall short in a January cold snap; auxiliary strips cover the gap.

Where the rule breaks

  • Very small rooms: below ~150 sq ft, available equipment sizes (5k window, 9k mini-split) dominate the math — you buy the smallest unit made regardless.
  • Kitchens and gyms: internal heat loads swamp the per-square-foot baseline; the +4,000 BTU kitchen bump is a floor, not a ceiling.
  • Extreme climates: 110°F Phoenix attics-top and −10°F Minnesota basements both fall outside the range's assumptions — real load-calc territory.
  • Multi-room zoned systems: loads add, but diversity doesn't — see the multi-zone section of the mini-split guide.

BTU vs tons vs watts: the units, translated

The same cooling capacity wears three costumes, and sales materials mix them freely: 1 ton of cooling = 12,000 BTU/hr ≈ 3.5 kW of heat movement (about 1 kW of electricity at SEER2 14). So "24,000 BTU" = "2-ton" = a unit drawing roughly 1,700 W while running. Electric resistance heat uses the raw conversion — 1,000 W of baseboard delivers 3,412 BTU/hr, which is why the baseboard watts guide reads so differently from cooling math. Keeping the units straight is half the battle in HVAC quotes; the other half is noticing that a quote sized only by square footage has skipped every adjustment in the table above.

Ceiling height deserves its own warning

It's the most commonly skipped factor because it's invisible from the floor. A 500 sq ft great room with a 16 ft cathedral ceiling is not "500 sq ft × 25" — it's 500 × 25 × 2 = 25,000 BTU, five times the flat-ceiling number for the same footprint. Warm air stratifies to the rafters, and the thermostat at chest height never sees it; ceiling fans set to winter mode push the stratified air back down and can shave a full size class off the requirement. If your space has any ceiling over 9 ft, measure it and apply the height multiplier before anything else — it's usually the biggest single adjustment in the entire calculation.
