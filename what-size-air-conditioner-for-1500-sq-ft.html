What Size Air Conditioner Do I Need for 1,500 Sq Ft?

Last updated: October 7, 2026

1,500 sq ft is a 6,000 BTU/hr step on the room-AC chart, from 24,000 up to 30,000.

Full breakdown: BTU per square foot.

For a smaller whole space on a different method, see what size AC unit for 1,000 sq ft. The numbers below are only the ENERGY STAR room air conditioner rows at 1,500 sq ft.

Quick answer: 1,500 sq ft is the boundary between 24,000 BTU/hr (1,400 up to 1,500 sq ft) and 30,000 BTU/hr (1,500 up to 2,000 sq ft). The step is 6,000 BTU/hr. Heavy shade stays with 24,000. A very sunny area, or more than two people regularly, moves to 30,000.

Put the step in scale. 30,000 minus 24,000 is 6,000 BTU/hr, and 6,000 BTU/hr is the whole capacity ENERGY STAR assigns to a 150 up to 250 sq ft room. Choosing the wrong side of 1,500 sq ft is not a small rounding error. It is an entire small-room unit of capacity. That is also why a central system for a 1,500 sq ft house should not be picked off this window-unit table.

How to size it

If the area is one room-sized zone cooled by room equipment, pick a side. Shade and ordinary occupancy: 24,000. Very sunny, or more than two people regularly: 30,000. Then apply the adjustment that matches, once.

10% of 24,000 BTU/hr is 2,400 BTU/hr (24,000 × 10%).

10% of 30,000 BTU/hr is 3,000 BTU/hr (30,000 × 10%).

Shaded lower path: 24,000 − 10% = 21,600 BTU/hr, because 10% of 24,000 is 2,400. Sunny upper path: 30,000 + 10% = 33,000 BTU/hr, because 10% of 30,000 is 3,000. One person beyond two on the upper row is 30,000 + 600 = 30,600 BTU/hr. Kitchen use on the lower row is 24,000 + 4,000 = 28,000 BTU/hr. Kitchen use on the upper row is 30,000 + 4,000 = 34,000 BTU/hr. That last sum equals the chart's 34,000 capacity for a still larger area, but here it is kitchen arithmetic, not a change of floor area.

Buying 30,000 for a shaded area that should have started at 24,000 and then dropped 10% leaves a unit that satisfies the thermostat before it dries the air. ENERGY STAR's phrase for that air is "cool, moist air will make the room feel damp and clammy."

The ceiling under these rows is 8-foot. Higher than that, select a room air conditioner with a higher CADR. The chart does not print a replacement capacity for the taller room.

Worked example: 1,500 sq ft through this site's calculator

The chart above is ENERGY STAR's room air conditioner table. The room BTU calculator on this site is a separate planning method with its own factors, listed on the methodology page: square feet × 25 BTU/hr, times ceiling height ÷ 8, times a sun factor and an insulation factor, plus 600 BTU/hr per window, plus 10%, rounded up to the next 500. Those factors belong to this calculator. They are not ENERGY STAR or Manual J figures.

Example inputs: a 1,500 sq ft single-story home with nine windows, an 8 ft ceiling, average sun, and average insulation.

  1. Floor area: 1,500 × 25 = 37,500 BTU/hr.
  2. Windows: 9 × 600 = 5,400 BTU/hr. Running total: 42,900.
  3. Add 10%: 42,900 × 1.10 = 47,190 BTU/hr.
  4. Round up to the next 500: 47,500 BTU/hr, or 4.0 tons.

That is above the largest single head in the calculator's mini-split tab (36k), so it points to zones: about 24,000 BTU/hr each with two zones, or 16,000 with three. Open this example in the calculator and change any input.

That is 31.7 BTU/hr per sq ft, against the ENERGY STAR rows of 24,000 and 30,000 BTU/hr for 1,500 sq ft. The two methods answer different questions. The calculator adds window load and a 10% margin to a whole-space estimate. The ENERGY STAR chart sizes a room air conditioner for the area it cools. At 1,500 sq ft, the spread from best case to worst case is 37,500 to 80,500 BTU/hr. Ceiling height and insulation move the result more than one window does, because they multiply the whole floor-area load. ENERGY STAR handles ceilings differently: its capacities assume an 8-foot ceiling, and for higher ceilings it says you may want a unit with a higher CADR. It does not publish a per-foot adder.

Occupants, kitchen, and climate at 1,500 sq ft

The calculator has no occupant input, and cooking heat is not an input either. ENERGY STAR's adjustments cover both as fixed amounts: 600 BTU/hr for each person beyond two who regularly uses the space, and 4,000 BTU/hr for a unit used in a kitchen. Because they are fixed, their weight depends on size. At 1,500 sq ft the kitchen adder is 17% of 24,000 and 13% of 30,000. Two extra regular occupants add 1,200 BTU/hr, which is 5% of 24,000 and 4% of 30,000.

Neither the chart nor the calculator has a climate input. DOE describes one ton (12,000 Btu) per 400 to 500 sq ft as an old estimate that "does not take into account local climate and how well a house is sealed and insulated," and says that in a very efficient house one ton can condition 800 to 1,000 sq ft. For 1,500 sq ft, those two statements work out to 3 to 3.75 tons (36,000 to 45,000 BTU/hr) and 1.5 to 1.88 tons (18,000 to 22,500 BTU/hr). Manual J brings climate in through outdoor design conditions, taken from Manual J Tables 1A/1B or ASHRAE weather data (ACCA). DOE adds that correct sizing is especially important in humid climates, where short-cycling leads to poor humidity control (DOE fact sheet).

Common mistakes at 1,500 sq ft

  • Landing on the wrong side of 1,500. The step from 24,000 to 30,000 is 6,000 BTU/hr, which is 25% more capacity. Re-measure before you buy on the upper row, splitting odd shapes into rectangles and triangles the way ENERGY STAR describes.
  • Buying a 30,000 BTU/hr window unit for a whole house. A 1,500 sq ft home with central air is a Manual J job. DOE says Manual J inputs include wall, ceiling, window, and floor areas, insulation, and envelope and duct air leakage, plus orientation, roof color, and occupancy (DOE fact sheet).
  • Using the ton rule. DOE's old one ton per 400 to 500 sq ft estimate gives 3 to 3.75 tons for 1,500 sq ft. In a very efficient house DOE says one ton can condition 800 to 1,000 sq ft, which would be 1.5 to 1.88 tons. That spread is why the rule is not a size.
  • Adding a size up "for future expansion." That is one of the reasons contractors gave for deliberate oversizing in the survey DOE cites. In humid climates, DOE says short-cycling from oversizing leads to poor humidity control (DOE fact sheet).

Frequently asked questions

What size air conditioner for 1,500 sq ft?

1,400 up to 1,500 sq ft is 24,000 BTU/hr. 1,500 up to 2,000 sq ft is 30,000 BTU/hr. Heavy shade supports the 24,000 start. Sun or more than two people regularly supports the 30,000 start.

How large is that boundary step?

30,000 − 24,000 = 6,000 BTU/hr. That difference equals the entire capacity of the 150 up to 250 sq ft row. Ten percent of 24,000 is 2,400, so the shaded lower path is 21,600 BTU/hr.

What if someone buys 30,000 BTU/hr for a shaded 1,500 sq ft area?

That skips the lower row and the shade cut. ENERGY STAR says a unit that is too big cools the room before it removes humidity, and "cool, moist air will make the room feel damp and clammy." The sunny upper path is the one that starts at 30,000 and then adds 10% to reach 33,000 BTU/hr.

Is this the right chart for a 1,500 sq ft house?

Only for a room air conditioner, and only on an 8-foot ceiling. A central system should get a load calculation on the HVAC load calculator. The 1,000 sq ft guide is a different method for a smaller whole space.
