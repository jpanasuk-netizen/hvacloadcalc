What Size Air Conditioner Do I Need for 800 Sq Ft?

Last updated: October 7, 2026

800 sq ft is inside the 18,000 BTU/hr row, which runs from 700 up to 1,000 sq ft.

Full breakdown: BTU per square foot.

The site's separate whole-space walkthrough is what size AC unit for 1,000 sq ft. This page stays on the ENERGY STAR room-AC row for 800 sq ft.

Quick answer: 800 sq ft is inside 700 up to 1,000 sq ft, so ENERGY STAR's room air conditioner capacity is 18,000 BTU/hr. Heavy shade takes 10% off that figure (16,200 BTU/hr). It does not drop the area into the 14,000 row, which ends at 700 sq ft.

How to size it

Modify 18,000 rather than shopping a different row. Shade and sun are 10% in opposite directions. People add 600 BTU/hr each beyond two. A kitchen adds 4,000 BTU/hr. Those last two can sit on top of a percent when both facts are true.

10% of 18,000 BTU/hr is 1,800 BTU/hr (18,000 × 10%).

Shaded 800 sq ft is 18,000 − 10% = 16,200 BTU/hr, which is still above 14,000. Sunny 800 sq ft is 18,000 + 10% = 19,800 BTU/hr, which is still below 21,000. One extra person is 18,000 + 600 = 18,600 BTU/hr. Kitchen use is 18,000 + 4,000 = 22,000 BTU/hr. A sunny kitchen is 18,000 + 10% + 4,000: 19,800 + 4,000 = 23,800 BTU/hr. Quote that stack only for a space that is both very sunny and a kitchen.

A unit that is too big for the shaded case cools the air and stops before the humidity is gone. ENERGY STAR's description of the result is that "cool, moist air will make the room feel damp and clammy."

If the ceiling is higher than 8-foot, the chart's own note is to select a room air conditioner with a higher CADR. Do not convert that sentence into a percent the table does not print.

Example inputs: a one-bedroom apartment of 800 sq ft with five windows, an 8 ft ceiling, average sun, and average insulation.

Ceiling height and insulation move the result more than one window does, because they multiply the whole floor-area load. ENERGY STAR handles ceilings differently: its capacities assume an 8-foot ceiling, and for higher ceilings it says you may want a unit with a higher CADR. It does not publish a per-foot adder.

Occupants, kitchen, and climate at 800 sq ft

The calculator has no occupant input, and cooking heat is not an input either. ENERGY STAR's adjustments cover both as fixed amounts: 600 BTU/hr for each person beyond two who regularly uses the space, and 4,000 BTU/hr for a unit used in a kitchen. Because they are fixed, their weight depends on size. At 800 sq ft the kitchen adder is 22% of 18,000. Two extra regular occupants add 1,200 BTU/hr, which is 7% of 18,000.

Neither the chart nor the calculator has a climate input. DOE describes one ton (12,000 Btu) per 400 to 500 sq ft as an old estimate that "does not take into account local climate and how well a house is sealed and insulated," and says that in a very efficient house one ton can condition 800 to 1,000 sq ft. For 800 sq ft, those two statements work out to 1.6 to 2 tons (19,200 to 24,000 BTU/hr) and 0.8 to 1 tons (9,600 to 12,000 BTU/hr). Manual J brings climate in through outdoor design conditions, taken from Manual J Tables 1A/1B or ASHRAE weather data (ACCA). DOE adds that correct sizing is especially important in humid climates, where short-cycling leads to poor humidity control (DOE fact sheet).

Common mistakes at 800 sq ft

  • Sizing one window unit for rooms behind closed doors. The chart is for the "area to be cooled." If the 800 sq ft is a 280 sq ft bedroom and a 520 sq ft living area, each area has its own row: 7,000 BTU/hr (250 up to 300) and 12,000 BTU/hr (450 up to 550). One 18,000 unit in the living room only counts the bedroom if the air can actually get there.
  • Using the old ton-per-square-foot rule as the answer. DOE calls one ton per 400 to 500 sq ft an old estimate that does not account for local climate or how well the house is sealed and insulated. For 800 sq ft it gives 1.6 to 2 tons, which is 19,200 to 24,000 BTU/hr.
  • Skipping past 18,000 to the next bigger row. The next capacity, 21,000, belongs to 1,000 up to 1,200 sq ft. Very sunny 800 sq ft is 19,800 BTU/hr, which is still under it.
  • Leaving the unit out of level. ENERGY STAR says to make sure the unit is level so the drainage system works effectively.

Frequently asked questions

What size air conditioner for 800 sq ft?

The ENERGY STAR row is 700 up to 1,000 sq ft at 18,000 BTU/hr. 800 sq ft is inside it, so the chart capacity is 18,000 BTU/hr before adjustments.

Will heavy shade move 800 sq ft down to 14,000 BTU/hr?

No. Heavy shade reduces 18,000 by 10%. Ten percent of 18,000 is 1,800, so the shaded target is 16,200 BTU/hr. The 14,000 row is only for 550 up to 700 sq ft.

What do extra people and a kitchen do to 18,000 BTU/hr?

One person beyond two adds 600 BTU/hr: 18,000 + 600 = 18,600 BTU/hr. Kitchen use adds 4,000: 18,000 + 4,000 = 22,000 BTU/hr. A very sunny room adds 10%, which is 19,800 BTU/hr.

How is this different from the 1,000 sq ft guide?

This page is only the room air conditioner chart, on an 8-foot ceiling. The 1,000 sq ft AC guide is a separate walkthrough. A central system should get a load calculation on the HVAC load calculator. Higher ceilings: select a higher CADR.
