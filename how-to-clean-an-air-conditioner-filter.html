How to Clean an Air Conditioner Filter

A clogged filter does not change the room's load. It stops the unit you already sized from delivering it.

Built by Jeremy Panasuk. Published October 3, 2026.

Quick answer: The U.S. Department of Energy says a clogged filter restricts airflow, which decreases efficiency and reduces the air conditioner's ability to cool. Routinely cleaning or replacing filters can lower the unit's energy consumption by 5 to 15 percent. During peak season, DOE's heating and cooling guide says to clean or replace filters monthly, and to follow the owner's manual for that model.

What DOE actually tells you to do

Energy Saver 101 puts the filter in the airstream so it can remove particles, and it repeats the failure mode twice: a clogged filter restricts airflow through the unit. The quickest cooling-energy save on that sheet is to clean and replace the filters regularly. The quantified claim on the same sheet is the 5 to 15 percent drop in the air conditioner's energy consumption when you routinely clean or replace them. That is an energy figure, not a promise that the room will suddenly need fewer BTU per hour.

The Guide to Home Heating and Cooling sets the calendar: clean or replace filters monthly during seasons of peak use. It also says the owner's manual should provide the details of upkeep, and that a regular maintenance schedule can reduce heating and cooling costs by 5 to 10 percent. Those two percentages are different measurements from different DOE documents. The 5 to 15 percent is specifically filters and the air conditioner's energy consumption. The 5 to 10 percent is a broader maintenance schedule and the heating-and-cooling bill. Do not add them together.

Basic maintenance on the Energy Saver sheet, beyond the filter, is still not a refrigerant job. Check the evaporator coil every year and clean it if it needs it. If the coil fins are bent, straighten them with a fin comb. On a split system, clear debris and leaves from the outdoor fan, compressor, and condenser. Pass a stiff wire through the drain channels now and then so they do not clog. On a window unit, inspect the seals so cool air is not leaking around the chassis. Hire a certified professional when the unit needs more than that basic list. If the refrigerant is low or leaking, DOE says to call a trained technician to repair the leak and recharge the system.

Model-specific steps — which way the filter slides, whether it is washable, what the manufacturer says about soap — live in the owner's manual DOE tells you to consult. This page does not invent a hose-down procedure the department did not publish.

Worked example: the load does not move when the filter does

Run a 200 sq ft room through this site's room-load function with an 8 ft ceiling, 2 windows, average sun (factor 1.00), and average insulation (factor 1.00). Shell = 200 × 25 = 5,000 BTU/hr. Windows = 2 × 600 = 1,200. Subtotal = 6,200. Raw load = 6,200 × 1.10 = 6,820 BTU/hr. Rounded up to the next 500, the planning result is 7,000 BTU/hr.

There is no filter input on that function, because a dirty filter is not heat the room gained. Calculate the same room again after you clean the filter and the arithmetic is identical: still 6,820 raw, still 7,000 rounded. What changed, if DOE's description holds, is the unit's ability to move air across the coil and therefore to deliver cooling. A room that feels warm on a clogged filter is not evidence that 7,000 BTU/hr was the wrong target. ENERGY STAR's chart, separately, puts 150 up to 250 sq ft at 6,000 BTU/hr before sun, shade, people, or a kitchen. That chart also has no "dirty filter" row. Both tools assume the unit can breathe.

Common mistake

Replacing the air conditioner because the room got warm in July, without pulling the filter first. Oversizing is the expensive version of that mistake. ENERGY STAR says a unit that is too large cools the air before it removes humidity. DOE's FEMP room-air-conditioner guidance says oversizing raises energy use and gives poor humidity removal from excessive on-off cycling. You can buy that problem while the original unit, with a clean filter, was already in the right class. The other mistake is opening the refrigerant circuit to "clean it properly." DOE draws the line at basic maintenance and sends refrigerant work to a trained technician.

Limits, and when to call a pro

Filter cleaning does not replace a load calculation, and this calculator's 7,000 BTU/hr result is not a Manual J. If the filter is clean, the coil is clean, the outdoor unit is clear, and the room still cannot hold the thermostat, then size the room on the calculator and, for anything whole-house, have a contractor run ACCA Manual J. Airflow that is still weak after a new filter can be a duct problem; DOE's guide says crushed or disconnected ducts keep conditioned air from arriving, and the duct CFM page is the planning math for that, not a sheet-metal design. Annual maintenance on Energy Saver 101 is there to improve comfort and efficiency and to prolong the unit. It is not a reason to add BTU.

Frequently asked questions

How often should an air conditioner filter be cleaned?

DOE's Guide to Home Heating and Cooling says to clean or replace filters monthly during seasons of peak use, and to consult the owner's manual for the unit's own upkeep. Energy Saver 101 says routinely cleaning or replacing filters can lower an air conditioner's energy consumption by 5 to 15 percent.

Does a dirty filter change the BTU the room needs?

No. The room load is the heat the room gains. A clogged filter, DOE says, restricts airflow and reduces the unit's ability to cool. The calculated load stays put. Clean the filter before you buy a larger unit.

When should a technician clean the air conditioner instead?

DOE says to hire a certified professional when the unit needs more than basic maintenance, and to call a trained technician if refrigerant is low or leaking. Do not open the refrigerant circuit to wash a coil.
