What Size Air Conditioner Do I Need for a 150 Sq Ft Room?

150 sq ft is the shared edge of the 5,000 and 6,000 BTU/hr rows, so the row choice comes before the 10% tweaks.

Full breakdown: BTU per square foot.

Quick answer: A 150 sq ft room is listed on two ENERGY STAR rows at once: 100 up to 150 sq ft needs 5,000 BTU/hr, and 150 up to 250 sq ft needs 6,000 BTU/hr. Choose 5,000 BTU/hr when the room is heavily shaded. Choose 6,000 BTU/hr when it is very sunny or more than two people are in it regularly.

Treat 150 sq ft as a tape-measure problem, not a guess. ENERGY STAR says to multiply length by width. Leftover inches convert as 3 in = .25 ft, 6 in = .5 ft, and 9 in = .75 ft. If the product of those measurements is still on the 100 up to 150 side, the row is 5,000 BTU/hr. If the inches push the area onto 150 up to 250, the row is 6,000 BTU/hr before any sun or shade tweak.

How to size it

Pick the row, then apply a single adjustment. Shade and sun are opposites: do not take 10% off and add 10% in the same pass. The chart does not tell you to average 5,000 and 6,000.

AdjustmentOn the 5,000 rowOn the 6,000 rowSource
Heavily shaded, reduce capacity 10%5,000 − 10% = 4,500 BTU/hr6,000 − 10% = 5,400 BTU/hrENERGY STAR
Very sunny, increase capacity 10%5,000 + 10% = 5,500 BTU/hr6,000 + 10% = 6,600 BTU/hrENERGY STAR
Each additional person beyond two5,000 + 600 BTU/hr = 5,600 BTU/hr6,000 + 600 BTU/hr = 6,600 BTU/hrENERGY STAR
Unit used in a kitchen5,000 + 4,000 BTU/hr = 9,000 BTU/hr6,000 + 4,000 BTU/hr = 10,000 BTU/hrENERGY STAR

10% of 5,000 BTU/hr is 500 BTU/hr (5,000 × 10%).

10% of 6,000 BTU/hr is 600 BTU/hr (6,000 × 10%).

A heavily shaded 150 sq ft bedroom should start on 5,000, then drop 10% to 4,500 BTU/hr. A very sunny 150 sq ft room should start on 6,000, then add 10% to 6,600 BTU/hr. On the 6,000 row, that sun swing is 600 BTU/hr, the same figure as the per-person adder, but they are different adjustments: one is 10% of the row, the other is 600 BTU/hr for each person beyond two.

One extra person on the shaded path is 5,000 + 600 = 5,600 BTU/hr, and you would still have started from the lower row because of the shade. One extra person on the sunny path is 6,000 + 600 = 6,600 BTU/hr. If this 150 sq ft space is actually a kitchen, add 4,000 after the row is chosen: 9,000 BTU/hr from 5,000, or 10,000 BTU/hr from 6,000. That kitchen case is worked in full on the kitchen air conditioner page.

The published chart is based on an 8-foot ceiling. ENERGY STAR's higher-ceiling note is a capacity step, not a percent: "If you have higher ceilings, you may want to select a room air conditioner with a higher CADR."

Buying the 6,000 row for a shaded room that belongs on 5,000 is the oversizing mistake in miniature. A unit that is too big cools the room before it removes humidity, and ENERGY STAR's result is that "cool, moist air will make the room feel damp and clammy."

Frequently asked questions

Does a 150 sq ft room need 5,000 or 6,000 BTU/hr?

Both rows name 150 sq ft. 100 up to 150 sq ft is 5,000 BTU/hr, and 150 up to 250 sq ft is 6,000 BTU/hr. Heavy shade is the reason to stay on 5,000. A very sunny room, or more than two people in the room regularly, is the reason to start at 6,000.

How do the shade and sun adjustments change a 150 sq ft room?

Reduce capacity 10% for a heavily shaded room and increase it 10% for a very sunny room. Ten percent of 5,000 is 500, so a shaded room that starts on the lower row lands at 4,500 BTU/hr. Ten percent of 6,000 is 600, so a sunny room that starts on the higher row lands at 6,600 BTU/hr.

What if more than two people use this room, or it is a kitchen?

The chart already covers up to two people. Each person beyond two adds 600 BTU/hr, so one extra person on the 6,000 row is 6,600 BTU/hr. A unit used in a kitchen adds 4,000 BTU/hr: 6,000 + 4,000 = 10,000 BTU/hr. The kitchen page works that adder on its own.

Can I use this chart for a central air conditioner?

No. This is a room air conditioner chart, and it assumes an 8-foot ceiling. A whole-house central system should get a load calculation on the HVAC load calculator. If the ceiling is higher than that 8-foot basis, ENERGY STAR says you may want a room air conditioner with a higher CADR.
