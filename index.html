How does this HVAC load calculator work?

Every tab here is built on the same skeleton, borrowed from ACCA Manual J and simplified to the inputs that move the number most. This calculator’s baseline is 25 BTU/hr per square foot. From there it applies four factors:

FactorAdjustment
Ceiling heightmultiply by height ÷ 8 (a 10 ft ceiling adds 25%)
Sun exposurevery sunny ×1.15 · average ×1.0 · shaded ×0.90
Insulationpoor ×1.25 · average ×1.0 · good ×0.85
Windows+600 BTU/hr each (single- to double-pane typical)

Then a 10% margin, rounded up to the nearest 500 BTU.

Sun factors are sunny ×1.15, average ×1.0, and shaded ×0.90. Insulation factors are poor ×1.25, average ×1.0, and good ×0.85. Those multipliers are this calculator’s, printed on this page.

How do you determine what size HVAC system you need?

The calculator above is one planning pass. The U.S. Department of Energy's Guide to Home Heating and Cooling describes a different, older estimate: one ton (12,000 Btu) of cooling for each 400 to 500 square feet of floor area. DOE says that rule does not account for local climate or how well the house is sealed and insulated. In a very efficient house, the same guide says one ton can condition 800 to 1,000 square feet. On a 1,000 sq ft house the old estimate is 2 to 2.5 tons (1,000 ÷ 500 = 2; 1,000 ÷ 400 = 2.5). The efficient-house range is 1 to 1.25 tons (1,000 ÷ 1,000 = 1; 1,000 ÷ 800 = 1.25). DOE says oversized cooling systems can be noisy, provide inadequate thermal comfort, and lead to mold and poor indoor air quality, and it says to insist that the contractor use ACCA Manual J.

This site's 25 BTU/hr per square foot path, walked through on the 1,000 sq ft page, is not the same rule as that ton-per-area estimate. Use the tabs for a room-by-room planning number. Do not treat either shortcut as the purchase. What BTU stands for is the unit on the quote. BTU to kW is the resistance-heat conversion, and it is not a furnace size by square footage.

Why is oversizing the #1 DIY HVAC mistake?

Bigger feels safer and isn't. An AC that's too big cools the air fast, shuts off before it has dehumidified anything, and you get a cold clammy room plus compressor short-cycling that kills the equipment years early. Inverter mini-splits modulate. A head far above the load is capacity you can't use. Undersizing means the unit runs longer on the worst days.

Common questions, answered

How many BTU do I need per square foot?

This calculator uses 25 BTU/hr per square foot, then multiplies by ceiling height ÷ 8, by a sun factor (sunny ×1.15, average ×1.0, shaded ×0.90), and by an insulation factor (poor ×1.25, average ×1.0, good ×0.85). Each window adds 600 BTU/hr. It then adds a 10% margin and rounds up to the nearest 500 BTU.

What size mini-split do I need for my room?

This calculator's indoor heads are 9,000, 12,000, 15,000, 18,000, 24,000, 30,000, and 36,000 BTU/hr. It picks the smallest head at or above the room load from that same function.

How do I calculate duct CFM from BTU?

CFM = BTU/hr ÷ (ΔT × 1.08). A 24,000 BTU/hr system at a 20°F split is 24,000 ÷ (20 × 1.08) = 1,111 CFM. At a 28°F split the same 24,000 BTU/hr is 24,000 ÷ (28 × 1.08) ≈ 794 CFM, about 397 CFM per ton.

How many watts of electric baseboard heat do I need per square foot?

This calculator converts with watts = BTU/hr ÷ 3.41 after it runs the room-load function. It does not use a watts-per-square-foot chart.
