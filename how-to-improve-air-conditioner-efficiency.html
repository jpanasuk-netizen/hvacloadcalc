How to Improve Air Conditioner Efficiency

Some of the gain is how you run the unit you have. Some of it is heat the room never picks up, which is the part a load calculation can see.

Built by Jeremy Panasuk. Published October 3, 2026.

Quick answer: DOE's Energy Saver 101 says the quickest cooling-energy save is cleaning and replacing filters, with a published 5 to 15 percent drop in the air conditioner's energy consumption. The same sheet says ceiling fans let you raise the thermostat about 4 degrees, and that fans cool people, not rooms. A separate DOE guide sets 78°F as the away setting in summer. Shade and insulation change the load itself. This calculator can show that second effect. It cannot show the filter percentage, because a filter is not an input.

Steps that have a DOE number

Keep the documents straight, because the percentages are not interchangeable.

  • Filters. Energy Saver 101: a clogged filter restricts airflow and reduces the unit's ability to cool. Routinely cleaning or replacing filters can lower the air conditioner's energy consumption by 5 to 15 percent. The Guide to Home Heating and Cooling says to do that monthly in peak season. Details are in the filter page.
  • Thermostat while you are gone. The heating and cooling guide says you can save as much as 10 percent on heating and cooling costs by adjusting the thermostat when you are away and while you sleep. For summer, when you are not home, it says set the thermostat to 78°F. ENERGY STAR's room-unit tips use the same 78°F as "as high as is comfortable, typically 78°F."
  • Fans. Energy Saver 101 says a ceiling fan lets you raise the thermostat about 4 degrees without hurting comfort, and to turn fans off when you leave because they cool people, not rooms. ENERGY STAR adds a room-unit version: on humid days set the fan speed low, because slower air movement removes more moisture, and use an extra fan to spread cooled air.
  • Sun and internal heat. The heating and cooling guide says to close drapes against summer sun. Energy Saver 101 says not to heat the house with appliances on hot days, and to use a bathroom fan when showering and a range hood when cooking so heat and humidity leave.
  • Ducts and the cabinet. Energy Saver 101 says air loss through ducts accounts for about 30 percent of a cooling system's energy consumption, and to seal them. The heating and cooling guide says to keep the outdoor unit clear of weeds and debris. ENERGY STAR says an improperly installed room air conditioner leaks as much air as a 6-square-inch hole, and to use the insulation shipped with the unit.
  • The machine itself. Energy Saver 101 says ENERGY STAR qualified air conditioners are, on average, up to 15 percent more efficient than standard models. That is a product comparison, not a reason to buy a larger capacity.

A programmable thermostat is the tool DOE's guide points at for the away setpoint. One relevant search link is below. It does not change the math.

Worked example: same room, two envelopes

DOE does not publish this calculator's sun and insulation multipliers. They are this site's rule of thumb, and they are the right tool for showing that the planning load moves when the envelope moves. Hold the room fixed: 200 sq ft, 8 ft ceiling, 2 windows. Run it twice.

Very sunny (×1.15) and poor insulation (×1.25). Shell = 200 × 25 × 1.15 × 1.25 = 7,187.5 BTU/hr. Windows = 1,200. Subtotal = 8,387.5. Raw = 8,387.5 × 1.10 = 9,226.25 BTU/hr. Rounded up to the next 500: 9,500 BTU/hr.

Shaded (×0.90) and good insulation (×0.85). Shell = 200 × 25 × 0.90 × 0.85 = 3,825 BTU/hr. Windows stay 1,200, because this function does not remove the glass when you add shade. Subtotal = 5,025. Raw = 5,025 × 1.10 = 5,527.5 BTU/hr. Rounded up: 6,000 BTU/hr.

The rounded planning loads differ by 9,500 − 6,000 = 3,500 BTU/hr. That gap is not DOE's 5 to 15 percent filter claim and it is not the 10 percent thermostat claim. It is what this calculator does when only sun and insulation change. ENERGY STAR's own sun adjustment, on the 6,000 BTU/hr row for 150 up to 250 sq ft, is 10 percent either way: 6,600 if the room is very sunny, 5,400 if it is heavily shaded. Smaller swing, different method, same direction. Shade lowers the shopping row. It also lowers this planning stack. Buying a larger air conditioner to cover a sunny, leaky envelope freezes that envelope into the equipment. Close the drapes, and if the insulation is the problem, that is a building fix, not a tonnage fix.

Common mistake

Stacking every percentage you can find. Filters at 5 to 15 percent, a maintenance schedule at 5 to 10 percent, a thermostat setback up to 10 percent, and an ENERGY STAR unit up to 15 percent more efficient than a standard model are four different comparisons. Adding them to "40 percent off my bill" invents a fifth number. The other mistake is upsizing because the room is uncomfortable, then never cleaning the filter or pulling the drapes. ENERGY STAR says the efficient room air conditioner is the one sized to the floor area, and that an oversized unit feels clammy because humidity removal loses the race to the thermostat.

Limits, and when to call a pro

Sealing ducts, straightening a system that is already installed, and any refrigerant work are past the basic list. Energy Saver 101 says to hire a certified professional when the unit needs more than basic maintenance, and the heating and cooling guide says many HVAC companies will seal ducts. Crushed ducts are in that same guide: conditioned air never arrives. This page's 3,500 BTU/hr gap is a calculator contrast, not a Manual J, and not a prediction of your utility bill. For a whole house, DOE says to insist on ACCA Manual J, because square-foot rules skip climate and how tight the house is. Run your own inputs on the calculator before you treat 9,500 or 6,000 as your number.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest DOE step for air conditioner energy use?

Energy Saver 101 says the quickest way to save energy on home cooling is to regularly clean and replace the filters, and that doing so can lower the air conditioner's energy consumption by 5 to 15 percent.

What thermostat setting does DOE suggest in summer?

DOE's heating and cooling guide says that when you are not home in summer, set the thermostat to 78°F. ENERGY STAR's room-air-conditioner tips say to set the thermostat as high as is comfortable, typically 78°F. The same DOE guide says you can save as much as 10 percent on heating and cooling by setting the thermostat back when you are away or asleep.

Do ceiling fans let you raise the thermostat?

Energy Saver 101 says ceiling fans let you raise the thermostat setting about 4 degrees without reducing comfort, and that fans cool people, not rooms, so they should be turned off when you leave.
