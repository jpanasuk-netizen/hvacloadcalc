How to Dispose of an Air Conditioner

The metal can be recycled. The refrigerant cannot be cut loose. EPA's rule does not scale with BTU.

Built by Jeremy Panasuk. Published October 3, 2026.

Quick answer: EPA prohibits intentionally venting ozone-depleting refrigerants or their substitutes while you maintain, service, repair, or dispose of air-conditioning equipment. For a window unit, which typically still holds its charge when it enters the waste stream, the final disposer must make sure the refrigerant is recovered first. Size the replacement before that unit leaves the house. Do not vent it to "get a head start."

What Section 608 requires

EPA's venting page, citing 40 CFR Part 82 Subpart F and Section 608 of the Clean Air Act, prohibits individuals from intentionally venting ozone-depleting substances or their substitutes during maintenance, service, repair, or disposal. The page allows only three kinds of releases. One is a "de minimis" quantity released in a good-faith attempt to recapture, recycle, or safely dispose of refrigerant, including releases when hoses are connected or disconnected to charge or service an appliance. The second is refrigerant emitted in normal operation, as opposed to during maintenance or disposal, such as mechanical purging and leaks — and EPA still requires repair of leaks above a certain size in equipment that contains 50 pounds of refrigerant or more. The third is a release of a substitute refrigerant that EPA has determined does not pose a threat to the environment. Emptying a household unit by cutting the process tube is not on that list.

The safe-disposal page splits appliances into two paths. Equipment that is typically dismantled on site before disposal — EPA's examples include central residential air conditioning, retail food refrigeration, chillers, and industrial process refrigeration — must have the refrigerant recovered under EPA's servicing requirements before disposal. Equipment that usually hits the waste stream still charged — EPA's examples include window unit air conditioners, household refrigerators and freezers, and motor-vehicle air conditioners — follows the special safe-disposal requirements.

On that second path, the final person in the chain (EPA names a scrap metal recycler or a landfill owner) is responsible for ensuring refrigerant is recovered before final disposal. If that person accepts an appliance that no longer holds a charge, they must keep a signed statement from whoever dropped it off. The statement has to include the name and address of the person who recovered the refrigerant and the date it was recovered. A contract with a regular commercial supplier can take the place of one-off statements. EPA says that contract option is for businesses, not for infrequent suppliers such as individuals and "peddlers." EPA does not accept a sticker as proof. A signed statement or a qualifying contract is the record.

EPA's April 2020 appliance-disposal fact sheet states the small-appliance numbers that sit under those rules. Before disposal of small appliances, including window air conditioners, the facility must recover 80 percent of the non-exempt refrigerant if the recovery equipment is pre-1993, or 90 percent if it is not, using certified recovery or recycling equipment; or evacuate the appliance to 4 inches of mercury vacuum; or hold a signed statement or contract that recovery already met the safe-disposal requirements. The same fact sheet says the requirements apply to refrigerants that contain ozone-depleting substances, such as HCFCs, and to non-exempt substitutes, such as HFCs, HFOs, and blends of them. Some refrigerants are exempt; the fact sheet names carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and water as examples and points to 40 CFR § 82.154(a) for the list. Do not assume a modern window-unit refrigerant is on the exempt list because the can says it is "lower GWP."

Individuals who recover refrigerant from small appliances while preparing them for disposal are not required to be certified technicians, EPA's disposal page says. The recovery equipment still has to meet the same performance standards as recovery equipment used for servicing. That is not permission to pierce the line in the driveway. DOE's room-air-conditioner purchasing page, updated December 2024, states the homeowner-facing version in one sentence: the Clean Air Act requires that the refrigerant be recovered from appliances prior to final disposal.

ENERGY STAR notes that room air conditioners use refrigerant, and that choosing a lower-global-warming-potential refrigerant (its examples include R-32, R-454B, and R-290) means a lower climate impact if a release happens. A lower impact if a release happens is not an exemption from the venting prohibition. Shop the refrigerant on the way in. Do not vent it on the way out.

Worked example: size the replacement, then move the old unit

Disposal day is a bad time to discover you do not know what you are buying next. Run the room you are about to leave without cooling through this site's room-load function before the truck arrives. Inputs: 200 sq ft, 8 ft ceiling, 2 windows, average sun (factor 1.00), average insulation (factor 1.00).

Shell = 200 × 25 = 5,000 BTU/hr. Windows = 2 × 600 = 1,200. Subtotal = 6,200. Raw load = 6,200 × 1.10 = 6,820 BTU/hr. Rounded up to the next 500, the planning result is 7,000 BTU/hr, which is 7,000 ÷ 12,000 = 0.58 ton on DOE's 12,000 Btu per ton. ENERGY STAR's chart, a different method, puts 150 up to 250 sq ft at 6,000 BTU/hr before shade, sun, people, or a kitchen. Use the chart if the replacement is a window unit. Use the calculator as the planning pass if you are comparing a mini-split. Neither number changes EPA's recovery percentage. A 5,000 BTU/hr window unit and a 3-ton central condenser are both appliances under the disposal rules above; the central one is in the "dismantled on site" path, and the window unit is in the "charge still inside" path.

Common mistake

Setting a window unit on the curb, or cutting the copper so the cabinet is lighter. Both skip the recovery step EPA assigns before final disposal. A sticker on the side does not replace the signed statement EPA describes. The other mistake is hauling the unit away and then guessing the replacement from the old nameplate. If the old unit was oversized, copying its BTU/hr repeats the humidity problem ENERGY STAR describes: a unit that is too large cools the room before it removes moisture. If it was undersized, copying it leaves the same shortfall. Calculate first, then hand the charged cabinet to a recycler or retailer set up to recover refrigerant.

Limits, and when to call a pro

This page is EPA's disposal rule plus one planning calculation. It is not a license to recover refrigerant, and it is not a Manual J. Central equipment that is cut out of a house on site has to be recovered under the servicing requirements, which means a technician and certified recovery equipment, not a homeowner with pliers. If you are unsure whether the refrigerant in a particular unit is an exempt substitute, stop and read 40 CFR § 82.154(a) or ask the disposer — do not guess that "new" means "legal to vent." For the replacement size, use the calculator as a planning tool only. A whole-house change-out still belongs with a contractor running ACCA Manual J, which DOE's heating and cooling guide tells you to insist on.

Frequently asked questions

Can I vent the refrigerant and throw an air conditioner away?

No. EPA regulations under Section 608 of the Clean Air Act prohibit individuals from intentionally venting ozone-depleting substances or their substitutes while disposing of air-conditioning equipment. Only three kinds of releases are permitted, and cutting the line to empty the unit is not one of them.

Who has to recover refrigerant from a window air conditioner?

EPA says the final person in the disposal chain, such as a scrap metal recycler or landfill owner, is responsible for ensuring refrigerant is recovered before final disposal. For small appliances such as window air conditioners, the fact sheet requires recovery of 90 percent of non-exempt refrigerant (80 percent with pre-1993 recovery equipment), or evacuation to 4 inches of mercury vacuum, or a signed statement that recovery already happened.

Does a lower-BTU replacement change the disposal rule?

No. The recovery requirement follows the appliance and the refrigerant, not the capacity number on the replacement. Size the new unit separately, then hand the old one to a disposer who recovers the charge.
